The Mainland Property Was Already Sold: What Should the Family Check First?

Do not seize keys, remove an occupant or sign a replacement document. Put the contract, payment, handover and registration dates around the date of death first.

Last updated：2026-08-03

Three points to keep in view

  • Place the transaction before or after death
  • Use four columns for the transaction
  • Trace the property and the price separately

Current registration alone does not answer whether the property remains in the estate

A property still registered to the deceased may have a contract, payment and possession history that must be considered. A completed registration also does not prevent the family from checking authority and where the price went.

Trace the date, signatory, actual payment, handover and registration before deciding whether the issue is the property, unpaid price or another surviving right.

Place every event around the date of death

For a lifetime agreement, check the registration, the deceased person's intention, payment and later handover. For a document appearing after death, identify who signed and under what representative basis.

Bank records, management charges, renovation, key handover, messages and actual occupation can rebuild an uncertain chronology.

One sale agreement is not the whole transaction

Use four columns: contract, payment, possession and registration. Record parties, property, price and conditions, then every payment with its payer and recipient.

Under possession, record keys, occupation, management charges and rent. Under registration, record the current holder, application, mortgage, restriction or other dealing.

Check what share the deceased could deal with

If the property was co-owned by spouses or others, identify the deceased person's actual share. One person's agreement does not necessarily dispose of every co-owner's interest.

Holding the title document, collecting rent or managing charges does not alone prove authority to sell.

Trace the property and sale proceeds separately

For unpaid price, record the due date, original recipient and who can now continue the transaction. If proceeds entered a relative's account, preserve the full trail rather than accepting that the person collected for everyone.

Do not assume that no registration means no transaction, or that completed registration ends every inquiry into payment and authority.

Preserve evidence when risk is rising

Two contract versions, different prices, missing proceeds, a resale, attachment or litigation require complete copies, original bank data and occupation records.

Until the facts are clear, do not sign retrospectively, collect the balance, resell the property or confront the occupant. The four-column table usually reveals which evidence is missing first.
