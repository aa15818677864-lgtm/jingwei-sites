Start with the situation itself

When someone will not sign inheritance or transfer papers, identify whether the dispute concerns heirship, the property share, the transfer plan or missing information. The person may dispute who the heirs are or how the estate should be divided. They may agree but avoid the document work. Or they may have received no clear explanation of the proposal. The next step depends on which problem is actually present.

Identify the document or decision they refuse

Are they withholding identity records, declining a power of attorney, challenging a will, opposing a sale, or rejecting the proposed payment? Naming the document and its consequence shows where the disagreement lies.

Some people are asked only to “sign here” without seeing the property list, title position, rent account or proposed ownership allocation. That may be an information problem. A person who says the whole property belongs to them presents a different evidential and legal issue.

Separate common facts from the allocation dispute

The family may be unable to agree on shares but still be able to confirm the death record, property address, registered owner, person holding the title papers and person collecting rent. Preserve these agreed facts before documents disappear or memories become less reliable.

Do not force every issue into one group chat. Work in stages: identify the heirs and property, account for rent, mortgage and management expenses, then discuss transfer, disposal and ownership shares. Keep a short written record of each stage.

Repeated pressure is not the same as progress

Daily demands for a signature often make the other person more defensive. Explain what the document does, what changes after signature, what alternatives exist and what remains undecided. Give a reasonable response period and record any conditions without promising an answer during an emotional telephone call.

Know when waiting has become risky

Do not rely indefinitely on informal discussion if a property may be sold, the registration may change, rent is unaccounted for, or one person openly denies everyone else's position. Preserve the evidence and assess whether a formal letter, mediation or court route needs consideration.

A refusal to sign does not automatically make the person unreasonable, and it does not mean the rest of the family must give up. Identify the precise decision first, then choose information, negotiation or formal action.
