Thinking of Renouncing a Mainland Estate? Check Three Accounts First

When a Mainland property appears difficult to deal with and family members disagree, a Hong Kong relative may say, “I do not want any of it; I will just sign a renunciation.”

Start with the situation itself

When a Mainland property appears difficult to deal with and family members disagree, a Hong Kong relative may say, “I do not want any of it; I will just sign a renunciation.” Before making that decision, check three accounts: what the estate may contain, what liabilities or expenses exist, and whether the family's plan will actually work after the renunciation.

First account: the full asset picture

Do not look at one property alone. Place property, savings, rent, employment-related payments, company interests and other credible clues in one list. A family may focus on an old flat and later discover savings or compensation. The opposite also happens: a stated property value ignores a mortgage, arrears and continuing management costs.

At minimum, understand the likely scope of what is being given up. An early statement made only in response to one asset may create difficulty when other assets emerge.

Second account: debts and family advances

Check loans, property charges, medical or funeral expenditure, and payments made by relatives to preserve or manage the estate. A person's claim that they paid everything should be supported by records and a clear calculation.

Renunciation should not be used as a shortcut to avoid checking the figures. Nor should a relative sign immediately because someone says there are large debts without showing the basis.

Third account: what happens afterwards

A family may expect one person to renounce so that another can deal with the asset alone. That may not simplify matters if there are other possible heirs, a minor, a missing person or more than one connected estate. The remaining family still needs a workable plan for signatures, management and receipt of funds.

Do not rely on a generic form

The wording and document route may depend on the asset, city and institution that will receive it. Names, identity, scope and intention must be clear. A form downloaded without reference to its intended use may be rejected or later questioned.

Renunciation changes a person's position. It should follow a review of the assets, liabilities and practical family plan, not simply a wish to avoid travel or end a difficult conversation.
