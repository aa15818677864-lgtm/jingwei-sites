A Rented Mainland Property After a Death: Who Should Receive the Rent?

A tenant may continue paying rent after the owner dies, only to receive different instructions from different relatives.

Start with the situation itself

A tenant may continue paying rent after the owner dies, only to receive different instructions from different relatives. One family member says to use the old bank account. Another says that all future rent must go elsewhere. The tenant does not know whom to follow, while the family starts asking whether earlier payments are missing.

The first task is not to divide the money. It is to preserve a reliable record of the lease, the payments and the property. If those three things remain scattered among several relatives, even a routine transfer or sale can turn into an argument over rent that nobody can reconstruct.

Do not ask the tenant to choose between relatives

A tenant should not have to decide which family member can speak for everyone else. Conflicting payment notices create a real risk of duplicate payment, payment to the wrong account, or the tenant stopping payment altogether.

Start by confirming which lease the tenant is following, the usual payment date, the account previously used, and who holds the deposit. If the payment arrangement genuinely needs to change, the tenant should receive one consistent written notice from a person who can explain the authority and the temporary arrangement.

The death of the owner is also not a reason to assume that the existing tenancy has simply disappeared. Before asking the tenant to leave or sign a replacement lease, check the term, deposit, repairs, renewal provisions and actual payment history.

Separate the property, the lease and the money trail

First, identify the property. Confirm the registered owner, whether anyone else already co-owned it, and whether the rented unit matches the registered address. If the property was already co-owned, work out what share actually falls into the estate before treating all rent as income from one person's estate.

Second, review the lease. Record the contracting parties, term, rent, deposit, renewal arrangements and responsibility for repairs. If the family has only photographs of a contract, messages or old receipts, place them in date order. Do not rush to create a new document that does not match what has actually happened.

Third, trace the payments. Export the bank records covering the period before and after the death. Match each payment to a rental month and separate ordinary rent from arrears, advance payments and the security deposit. A relative who collected rent in the past should also provide the corresponding payment and expense records. Acting as the collector does not, by itself, decide who ultimately receives the money.

Build a ledger another family member can audit

The rent ledger does not need to be a complicated accounting system. It should, however, answer five basic questions:

  • Which rental month does the payment cover, and when was it received?
  • How much was paid, into which account, and what receipt or transfer record exists?
  • What amounts represent the deposit, arrears or rent paid in advance?
  • Who paid management charges, repairs and other necessary property expenses?
  • What notice was sent to the tenant, when was it sent, and how did the tenant respond?

Suppose the family receives RMB 12,000 in July. That may be RMB 10,000 for July and RMB 2,000 of June arrears. An entry that says only “July rent: RMB 12,000” will be difficult to explain several months later.

Keep the ledger and supporting documents somewhere the relevant family members can review. If the family has agreed a traceable interim arrangement, one person may collect rent and pay necessary expenses under that arrangement. Each transaction should still be recorded, and the rental income should not be mixed with that person's everyday spending.

Temporary collection is not early distribution

An interim arrangement should identify the tenant contact, the receiving account, the expenses that may be paid, and how often an account will be given to the family. Its purpose is to protect the property income and preserve evidence. It does not decide who inherits the property or how the rent will ultimately be divided.

Final treatment of the rent depends on the original ownership of the property, any co-owned share, the people involved in the estate, the lease and properly recorded expenses. If the family has not yet completed the property and document checks needed before a transfer, distributing the collected rent is usually premature.

Know when informal coordination is no longer enough

Move beyond family messages if:

  • different relatives have already sent conflicting instructions to the tenant;
  • someone refuses to provide the lease, bank records or deposit details;
  • the tenant is in arrears, plans to leave, or is asking for the deposit back;
  • a relative renews the lease, reduces the rent or takes a large advance payment without agreement; or
  • the property is about to be transferred or sold while ownership is disputed.

If the problem is only disorganised paperwork, begin with the lease, bank records and an expense ledger. If a family member is actively refusing to cooperate, identify whether the disagreement concerns accounting, temporary management or ownership of the property itself.

Three documents are enough to start: the latest lease, the payment records covering the period around the death, and a list of the deposit and property expenses. Once the family can explain where the money came from and where it went, it can make a better decision about who should manage the rent next.
