A Relative Is Living in the Estate Property: What Should the Other Heirs Check First?

A Hong Kong family may return to Mainland China and discover that a relative is still living in the deceased person's property.

Start with the situation itself

A Hong Kong family may return to Mainland China and discover that a relative is still living in the deceased person's property. The relative has the keys, keeps personal belongings there and says that they have paid the management charges and repairs for years. The immediate reaction is often: “Have they taken over the property, and can we make them leave?”

Do not begin by labelling the relative an unlawful occupier. Living in the property is only the visible fact. Why the person moved in, what the deceased agreed, whether the property was already co-owned and what the rest of the family knew will shape the next step.

Start with the reason the relative moved in

The resident may already be a co-owner. They may have rented the property from the deceased. They may have moved in to provide care with the deceased's permission. Or they may have been allowed to stay for a short period, with nobody revisiting the arrangement after the death.

Those situations look similar from outside, but they do not lead to the same answer. Collect the ownership record, any old lease, messages and payment evidence. Ask when the person moved in, who knew about it and whether the family ever discussed a term, rent or other condition.

If the title document cannot be found, begin with the address, old receipts and management records. A relative's statement that “the deceased promised me the home” should not decide the matter on its own. It should still be recorded and checked against other documents and witnesses rather than dismissed immediately.

Keep five histories separate

The first is the property history: who is registered, whether the property was co-owned, when it was acquired and whether there is evidence of shared payment. If it was co-owned before the death, identify the share that actually belongs to the estate.

The second is the occupation history: when the relative moved in, whether the deceased agreed, and whether there was rent, a time limit or another condition.

The third is the care history. Record what care was provided and whether the family discussed housing in return. “I provided the most care” may be an important fact, but it is not a complete answer to ownership.

The fourth is the expense history: management charges, utilities, repairs and renovations. Separate the resident's ordinary living expenses from money genuinely spent preserving or improving the property.

The fifth is the income history: whether any room was rented out, who received the rent, and whether the property was sublet or used for a business.

Putting those histories on one timeline makes the accounts easier to compare. A person who occupied the home for years may also have paid significant expenses. A relative who never lived in Mainland China may hold the original purchase and registration papers. Neither fact should be treated as the whole case.

Long occupation does not automatically transfer ownership

Time spent in the property does not replace evidence of original ownership or the arrangement made by the deceased. Equally, the fact that the resident is not on the title does not automatically mean that market rent has been due from the first day of occupation.

The original permission, the family's knowledge, the resident's housing circumstances and the point at which an objection was first raised can all matter. A claim for occupation charges also depends on the basis of occupation, any agreement and the available evidence. Applying current market rent to many past years without those checks may only add another dispute over care, repairs and family expenses.

A more useful approach is to separate the past from the future. First establish how the occupation began and what was paid. Then discuss whether the person may remain for a period, should move out by an agreed date, or wishes to take the property as part of a broader estate arrangement.

Avoid steps that create a second dispute

Do not change the locks while the resident is away. Do not disconnect utilities, remove belongings or rent the property to someone else before the facts and authority are clear. Those steps do not establish who owned the property and may create new disputes over possessions, occupation and expenses.

Likewise, a family relationship document alone does not show that one relative may demand all keys and records from the property manager. Being a family member and having present authority to deal with the property are different questions.

If there is a concern that the property is being sublet, altered or disposed of, preserve photographs of its current condition, management records and communications. Then assess whether a more formal response is needed. Avoid an irreversible step taken at the most emotional point of the disagreement.

Break the family discussion into three decisions

First, decide who will safeguard the property for now and how other family members may inspect it. Second, account for past management charges, repairs and any rental income, leaving disputed items clearly marked. Third, decide whether the future plan is continued occupation, a move-out date, transfer to the resident as part of the estate arrangement, or formal dispute assessment.

If the resident is willing to share records, the family can first establish a common set of facts. If access and accounting are refused, or another family member is openly opposing all further steps, the matter should not be left indefinitely in a family chat.

Start with a simple identity-and-timeline sheet. Record the ownership documents, reason for moving in, care arrangement, payments and income. A family that can see the same facts is in a better position to discuss occupation and expenses than one that begins by trading accusations.
