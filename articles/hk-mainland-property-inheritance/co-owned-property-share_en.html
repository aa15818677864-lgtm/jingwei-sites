A Property Was Co-owned: What Part Actually Falls Into the Estate?

A deceased person's name on a title does not always mean the entire property is part of the estate.

Last updated: 2026-07-23

This may be close to what your family is facing

  • Check registration and the history of acquisition
  • Do not use occupation as a substitute for ownership
  • Define the estate share before discussing division

Start with the situation itself

A deceased person's name on a title does not always mean the entire property is part of the estate. Equally, a title in one name should not be used to dismiss every other ownership issue before the purchase and family history have been checked. Deal with co-owned property in two layers: the share held before death, and the inheritance of that share afterwards.

Check registration and the history of acquisition

Record the registered owners, stated form of co-ownership, purchase date and payment sources. Was the property acquired during a marriage, paid for by several family members, or received through an earlier family arrangement? Collect contracts, transfers, gifts and allocation records without asking relatives to settle the issue from memory.

Do not use occupation as a substitute for ownership

Long occupation, payment of management charges and responsibility for repairs are relevant management and expense facts. They do not automatically decide the legal share. A relative who never lived in Mainland China should not be removed merely because of absence.

Keep occupation, rent, repairs and purchase payments as separate entries. Do not combine “who cared for the property” with “who originally owned it.”

Define the estate share before discussing division

If several people owned the property before the death, identify the deceased person's part before calculating how that part may pass. A common mistake is to divide the full market value among the heirs, even though only a portion belongs to the estate.

Include the original co-owners in the disposal plan

Even if the heirs agree among themselves, the original co-owners may have a separate position on use, sale or physical division. Put original owners and possible heirs on the same people chart before promising that one person can retain or sell the whole property.

Use two tables

The first table records the original ownership: registration, acquisition date, payment and co-ownership clues. The second records the inheritance: date of death, possible heirs and their positions. Their intersection identifies the share that needs to be handled.
