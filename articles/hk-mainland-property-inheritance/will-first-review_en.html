Start with the situation itself

After finding a will, verify its version and signing history, then check whether it actually covers the Mainland property and share that need to be transferred. Difficulties often appear later: there are two versions, the family has only a photograph, nobody can explain the signing, or the document mentions one property but says nothing about rent, a co-owned share, later property or relocation rights. Treat the will as an important record that still needs a factual review.

Identify exactly which document you have

Arrange every version by date. Record who holds the original, when and where it was found, whether it appears to have been altered, and who had seen it before. Do not keep only the version that best supports one person's understanding, and do not write on or re-bind the original.

If there is only a photograph or photocopy, preserve the original electronic file and the date it was received. Then investigate where the original may be. The history of the document can be as important as its wording.

Support the signing history with facts

Record who was present, where the signing occurred, the person's health and ability to communicate at the time, whether anyone witnessed it, and whether someone wrote or translated it. An honest gap is safer than a complete-sounding story that later conflicts with medical, travel or communication records.

Compare every name, property address, title reference and stated share with current records. An address or registration status may have changed over time.

List the property interests the will does not address

A will may deal with one flat but omit rent, a co-owned share, property acquired later or relocation rights. It may state a desired allocation without explaining the mortgage, management expenses or rent already received by a family member. Place the wording of the will beside the actual property list so that omissions are visible.

Preserve evidence before confronting a dispute

If a relative questions authenticity, signing or the deceased person's wishes, preserve the document, messages, medical and daily-life timeline, and the details of who first obtained it. An immediate confrontation can make records harder to recover.

A will is not a pass that removes the need to check identity, property and procedure. Review its version, source, signing and property coverage before deciding how it should be used.
