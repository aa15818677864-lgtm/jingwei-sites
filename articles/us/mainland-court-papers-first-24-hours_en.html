Court Papers Arrived from Mainland China: What to Do First

An email bearing a Mainland court name, an electronic-service link or a package forwarded by a relative can trigger one immediate question: “How many days do I have?” A fixed countdown is not a safe starting point until you know what the documents are, how they reached you and which date matters.

Last updated: 2026-07-27

Four points to remember

  • The first 24 hours are for triage, not a legal grace period
  • Preserve original files, envelopes and the delivery trail
  • Keep receipt, document and next-action dates separate
  • Source verification does not decide service or deadlines

The practical answer

Use the first 24 hours to preserve the original delivery trail, identify the court, case number, parties and document names, separate the relevant dates, and get the material to counsel who can check the Mainland case. Do not delete files, answer the underlying allegations, admit a debt or follow an unfamiliar payment link before the source and documents have been reviewed.

The 24-hour frame is a triage window, not a legal grace period. If a hearing or filing date is today or has already passed, or the papers suggest an immediate risk to property, accounts or evidence, preserve what you received and seek advice at once. Do not wait until every item below is complete.

If you only have your phone, do these six things

Checklist:
• Save the original PDF, full email or text message, and the front and back of any envelope. A screenshot is not enough.
• Photograph or write down the court name, case number, parties and the title of every document.
• Record who received or downloaded each item, when it happened and by what method.
• Put every stated hearing, filing or contact date on a separate line.
• Stop any unfamiliar payment, verification-code or unknown-app request.
• Send the originals and those five lines to counsel who can verify the Mainland matter.

If a stated date is today or has passed, finish preserving the files and make contact. You do not need to read the rest of this article first.

Preserve the delivery trail, not just the first page

For paper delivery, keep the complete envelope, shipping label, tracking history, signature information and the date and time you actually obtained the package. If it first went to a relative in Mainland China, a company reception desk or an old address, record each handoff. Do not replace the original delivery date with the day someone forwarded the papers to you.

For electronic delivery, save the original PDF, the full email including sender details, the text message, download notice and platform screen. Do not print and rescan the file as your only copy. Do not rename, annotate or remove pages from the original. For a shared company inbox, note who first saw or opened the message and when.

Those records do not decide the legal effect of delivery by themselves. They give counsel the facts needed to examine it.

Do not call every court document a summons

Read the heading and title of each item. A package may contain a complaint, a notice to respond, evidence, a hearing notice, a procedural order, a judgment, enforcement papers or a request to update contact details. Each document does a different job.

Create a one-page index showing:

Checklist:
• the full court name and the division or office handling the matter;
• the case number, case name and every party;
• the role assigned to you or the company;
• the title, page count and date of each document;
• every hearing, filing, contact or other stated date; and
• any verification information, seal or signature block.

Do not rely on an email subject line or a few words in a text message. If the party names, case number or dates in an attachment do not match the notification, mark the mismatch. Do not choose the version that merely looks more plausible.

Keep three clocks separate

The first clock is when the material arrived, was downloaded or was first seen in the United States. Record the local date, time, state and time zone.

The second clock is the set of dates printed or recorded on the papers: issue, dispatch, delivery or another event. The third is any hearing date, submission date or next step expressly stated in the documents.

An email reaching an inbox, a person opening a link, a relative forwarding a file and you reading it carefully are not necessarily the same event. Record them separately. Keep both U.S. local time and the corresponding Mainland time when possible.

Do not use a generic online deadline to start your own countdown. The type and stage of the case, the document involved, the delivery history and any existing representative can change the analysis. Verifying the source also does not determine what legal effect the receipt has or when a deadline begins. Ask counsel to identify the earliest date or task that must be addressed.

Verify the source without following an unfamiliar payment instruction

A court name on a document is not a reason to skip verification. An unfamiliar delivery method is not proof that the material is fake. Use a court's public page, a known court service platform or contact information already established in the case. Do not rely only on a private number that suddenly appears in a text or attachment.

Where an electronic document can be checked against a court service platform, keep the original file and the verification result. That comparison may help show whether the file matches the platform's retained version. It does not, by itself, decide the legal effect of delivery, calculate a deadline or establish that the claims in the case are correct.

Stop and verify through a separate channel if someone asks for immediate payment to a personal account, a verification code, installation of an unknown app or other sensitive credentials. During an initial verification call, do not volunteer bank passwords, full identity files or a long account of the dispute to an unverified caller.

Give counsel a one-page fact sheet

The point of a fact sheet is not to decide the case. It prevents the first review from turning into a search through dozens of screenshots.

Use six boxes:

Checklist:
• Recipient: individual, current company, former company, relative, reception desk or representative.
• Delivery path: paper, email, court platform, text message or a chain of handoffs.
• Case details: court, case number, parties and document titles.
• Dates: receipt, download, forwarding, document dates and the next stated event.
• Names and language: every Chinese and English name, company name, former name or prior identification used.
• Existing records: key contracts, payments, communications, company records and any prior Mainland counsel.

Mark a missing item as “to be confirmed.” Do not recreate old messages, backfill dates, delete unfavorable material or ask several people to align their accounts. Preserving the record as it exists is more useful than producing a polished story on day one.

Four questions matter because you are in the United States

First, is the named party the U.S. individual, the U.S. company or a related Mainland entity? Similar names do not make them the same defendant or participant.

Second, was Mainland counsel, company legal staff or an old address already designated to receive material? Third, who has authority to make decisions and provide records for the company? Fourth, if Mainland counsel must be instructed now, who will sign, what authority is needed and in what form?

Receipt in the United States may also raise a cross-border service question. Do not decide that the process is valid or invalid merely from the destination, courier brand or electronic platform. The document type, actual route, recipient and every handoff need to be reviewed together.

Mistakes that make the first day harder

Checklist:
• Keeping one photograph of the cover page but discarding the envelope, attachments or download record.
• Providing a code, identity document or payment before verifying the recipient.
• Keeping only a machine translation and losing the original text and page numbers.
• Admitting liability or promising payment in an attempt to buy time.
• Applying one online deadline to every paper in the package.
• Assuming that being in the United States means the matter can be ignored, or that every demand must be accepted immediately.

You do not need to solve the case on the first day. Preserving the originals, source, roles and dates, then identifying the earliest required action, is meaningful progress.

Ask counsel these three questions first

Send the original documents, envelope or electronic notice, the one-page fact sheet, the core contract and the most recent relevant communications in one folder. Ask:

Checklist:
• What is each document, and what stage has the case reached?
• How should the delivery record be examined, and what is the earliest date or task to address?
• With the decision-makers and records in the United States, which authority, translation or filing step should be prepared first?

Those answers should come before a full response, evidence project, procedural application or cross-border signing package. On day one, clarity about the papers and the next required action is more valuable than preparing documents for a purpose that has not yet been confirmed.
