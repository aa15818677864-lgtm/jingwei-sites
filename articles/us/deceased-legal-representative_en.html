The Mainland Company's Legal Representative Has Died: Separate the Two Tracks

A U.S. estate representative does not automatically take over a Mainland company office. Restore company decision-making, then trace the deceased person's separate rights.

Last updated: 2026-07-25

Three roles, three questions

  • Legal representative: an office held for the company
  • Director or manager: appointed through company governance
  • Shareholder: ownership must be proved separately

The company transition and the estate are connected, not interchangeable

If a relative dies in the United States while still named as the legal representative of a Mainland company, do not treat the U.S. estate representative, the company's legal representative and its shareholder as the same person. A court appointment may authorise someone to manage the estate, but it does not place that person in a company office or let them sign for the company.

The company must use its articles and current governance records to appoint a new officeholder and update the registration. Whether the deceased owned shares, and who may succeed to them, requires a separate review of the shareholder register, articles and estate evidence.

1. A name on the business licence does not prove ownership

The legal representative is a company office. A director, manager and shareholder each hold a different role. A business card, licence or public company page may show only one role, so the deceased person's name on the registration cannot establish that they owned all or part of the company.

Look for the articles, shareholder register, appointment records and the latest company decision. If there is no ownership evidence, separately check salary, loans, reimbursements and other personal receivables. Company property is not automatically estate property.

2. First identify who can make a valid company decision

Review which director or manager may serve as legal representative under the articles, and whether the remaining shareholders, directors or other authorised people can make the appointment. The application, company decision, appointment record and new officeholder's identity should tell one consistent story.

If the remaining company bodies can act, the officeholder change may proceed without waiting for the entire share succession. If the deceased was also the sole shareholder, records are stale or the parties disagree, confirm the local filing route first. Do not backdate documents or let an unauthorised person use the seal or company account.

3. U.S. estate papers answer the estate question

A death certificate, will and court appointment can establish the death, the testamentary plan and who may administer the estate. They may not answer who can call a company meeting, make an appointment or qualify as the new legal representative under the company's own arrangements.

The reverse is also true. Completing the company change does not distribute the deceased person's shares to a relative. The ownership track still requires evidence of the holding, the applicable articles, the shareholder register, the other heirs and the estate representative's authority.

4. Build two working sheets before the first call

The company sheet should identify who holds the articles, licence and seal, the current directors and shareholders, and any contract, payroll, tax or bank matter awaiting a valid signature. The estate sheet should list share evidence, distributions, loans, personal receivables and the status of the U.S. court appointment. Give every item a source and date.

On the first call, state the deceased person's registered office, whether any ownership evidence has been found, whether the current company participants can be reached, and which company matter is urgent. Separating role from ownership is usually more useful than preparing every cross-border document at once.
