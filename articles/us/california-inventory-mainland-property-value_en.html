A Mainland Home in a California Probate Inventory: Which Value and Which Share?

Purchase price, date-of-death value and today's asking price are three different numbers.

Last updated: 2026-07-25

Answer four questions first

  • Which Mainland property is involved
  • What interest the deceased person held
  • Which date the value must reflect
  • Where the current figure came from

Separate the date, the ownership interest and the debt

If the California matter requires the Mainland home to appear in a formal inventory, the valuation generally starts with the date of death. Confirm that date before looking for reliable evidence of value around it. The old purchase price explains acquisition history; a current asking price does not establish the earlier value.

The value of the whole home is not automatically the value of the deceased person's estate interest. A mortgage or other debt is another figure again. Record all three separately instead of producing an unverified net number.

1. Do not work backwards from today's listing price

A family may find the original purchase price, market information from around the death and a current listing at the same time. Put each figure beside its date and source. They answer different questions.

If a formal valuation is not yet available, record what the family actually has, the currency and what remains uncertain. Do not invent a round number to complete a form. The lawyer or other person handling the California matter should confirm what valuation evidence that case requires.

2. The whole property's value is not the deceased person's share

Check the current Mainland record, every registered owner, the interest shown for the deceased person and any mortgage or other debt. Keep the property's gross value, the deceased person's ownership interest and the debt as separate entries.

Even if the record names only the deceased person, do not assume that the entire value belongs in the estate without checking the marriage and acquisition history in the California matter.

3. Start with four questions, not a preferred number

On one page, identify the home, the interest the deceased person may have held, the valuation date and the source of each figure. For a non-dollar amount, also preserve the original currency and the basis used for any conversion. Mark gaps as unconfirmed.

If two sources differ sharply, keep both with their dates. Do not choose the most convenient figure or treat a family recollection as an appraisal. Let the case handler decide whether a formal appraisal or further evidence is needed.

4. The California inventory does not retitle the Mainland home

Where the asset must be reported, the California inventory helps that proceeding identify the property and its value. Whether and how foreign property belongs in a particular filing depends on the scope of that case. A dollar figure does not change the Mainland property record.

The Mainland process still needs the property's location, registered owner, title reference, heirs and proposed shares. If the California inventory omitted the home or misstated an address or interest, preserve the filed version and ask whether a supplemental or corrected inventory is appropriate.
