Which California Death Certificate Copy Should You Use?

A California death certificate should not be ordered for a Mainland estate simply by asking for a “certified copy.” California issues authorized and informational certified copies, but they are not interchangeable. An authorized copy can be used to establish the identity of the person named in the record. An informational copy carries a warning that it is not valid for that purpose and may have some information concealed.

The practical answer

Before ordering or apostilling anything, answer three questions: What does the Mainland recipient need the record to prove? Is the applicant eligible for an authorized copy? Which official signed the certificate? The first two questions determine the suitable copy. The signature at the bottom determines whether an additional county step may be needed before the California apostille.

Choose the copy by purpose, not by the word “certified”

An authorized certified copy is restricted to eligible applicants. Depending on the applicant, eligibility may arise from a close family relationship, appointment to act for the estate, legal representation of the estate or another recognised capacity. A mail request generally requires the applicant to complete the prescribed sworn statement and have it notarised.

A certified informational copy is still issued as a certified copy of the record. It can help a family confirm names, dates, place of death and other recorded details. It is not, however, valid for establishing the identity of the person named on the certificate. Some informational copies may also conceal information.

If a Mainland property registry, bank or court needs the record as part of an identity chain, do not assume that an informational copy will be treated like an authorized copy. Send the recipient a full scan showing the warning and any redactions, then ask what copy it requires for that particular use.

The sworn statement is part of the copy request

The sworn statement used to obtain an authorized copy addresses the applicant's eligibility to receive a restricted vital record. It does not establish the heirs to a Mainland estate and does not replace evidence of kinship, a will, an executor's authority or evidence about the Mainland asset.

An applicant who is not a listed close relative should first identify the capacity in which the request is made. An estate representative, a lawyer acting for the estate or a person appointed by a court may need to attach evidence of that authority. Translating the copy-request statement does not turn it into an inheritance declaration for use in Mainland China.

Read the signature at the bottom before seeking an apostille

The additional steps do not depend on where the family now lives. They depend on the issuing source and the official whose signature appears on the certificate.

A formal copy signed by a county clerk, county recorder or the State Registrar can generally be presented for a California apostille. A copy signed by a local health officer or local registrar may first require certification by the county clerk in the county of issue. The family can also compare the cost and time of obtaining a new copy from the county recorder or state vital-records office with a signature that can be authenticated directly.

That creates two practical routes:

Checklist:
• Keep the local copy and complete the required county certification.
• Obtain a county-recorder or state-issued copy, then apply for the apostille.

A photocopy does not become an official vital-record copy merely because a notary certifies another document around it. The word California on the page also does not show that the signature is one the apostille office can authenticate without an earlier step.

County of death and record year affect where you order

Local vital-record offices commonly hold more recent records. The county recorder can issue records for deaths registered in that county across a broader range of years, and the state vital-records office can issue California records through its own route.

Prepare these facts before applying:

Checklist:
• the deceased's name as recorded;
• date and county of death;
• the applicant's relationship or representative capacity;
• whether an authorized or informational copy is requested;
• the intended cross-border estate and apostille use.

If the county or year is uncertain, use funeral records, medical papers, old correspondence and family files to narrow the search. Sending speculative applications to several counties often creates delay without resolving which record is needed.

An apostille will not cure a name mismatch

An apostille authenticates the source signature, the official's capacity and the relevant seal or stamp. It does not prove that an English name and an older Chinese name refer to the same person. It also does not decide who inherits a Mainland property or bank balance.

Compare the death certificate with passports or state identification, old Mainland identity or household records, property or bank records, and the recorded dates and places. If the death record itself is wrong, ask the issuing or record-holding office about amendment. If the record is correct but the names differ across jurisdictions, prepare a separate name-linking file.

A translation should reproduce the informational-copy warning and any visible limitations. A translator should not invent a Chinese name or a family relationship to make the documents appear consistent.

Ask the Mainland recipient what the record must prove

A property registry, bank, company and court can each use a death certificate for a different purpose. Give the actual recipient the copy type, issuing office, county of death, signer and proposed evidential purpose. Then ask whether it requires an authorized copy, apostille, full Chinese translation, name-linking evidence or other family documents.

For a qualifying California public document used in Mainland China, the apostille route normally replaces the older consular-legalisation chain. That simplification concerns the document's origin. The recipient may still examine copy type, contents, translation, kinship and ownership evidence.

Stop before mailing if any of these remain unclear

Checklist:
• The receiving purpose has not been identified.
• The authorized or informational marking has not been checked.
• The recipient has not reviewed an informational-copy warning or redaction.
• The applicant's eligibility and sworn statement are incomplete.
• The signer's title has not been read.
• Names, dates or county of death do not match the rest of the file.
• No complete scan, page record or custody note has been made.

The sound order is: confirm the receiving purpose, choose the copy type, establish applicant eligibility, identify the issuing office, read the signer, complete any necessary county certification or obtain a better-suited copy, seek the California apostille, and only then finalise translation and name-linking evidence.
