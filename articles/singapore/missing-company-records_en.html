Missing Mainland Company Records? Build Two Evidence Lists First

First identify the right company. Then test whether the deceased still owned shares. A company search screenshot cannot answer both questions.

Last updated: 2026-07-25

Use two lists, not one conclusion

  • Company list: name, city, identifier and status
  • Ownership list: payments, agreements and distributions
  • For every clue: source, date and open question

Treat the search result as a lead, not proof of ownership

A business card, old contract or unexplained transfer may point to a Mainland company, but it does not establish what the deceased owned at death. Separate the identity of the company from the ownership question. Public company information can help with the first task; it rarely completes the second.

Create a company-identification list and a family-held ownership list. Put both in date order. Their gaps will show whether the next request should be for a registration archive, records held by the company, or documents connecting the deceased to the claimed interest.

1. Confirm the company before tracing the shares

Record the full legal name, registration city and unified identifier. Keep former names as well. A shop name, brand or office address may point to the business without identifying the company that issued the shares.

Do not stop simply because the company is shown as deregistered, revoked or inactive. That status describes the company now. Historical ownership, liquidation distributions and other amounts owed still need a separate check.

2. Keep the three record layers separate

Public company information, the registration archive, and records held by the company serve different purposes. Public information narrows the search. Access to a fuller archive depends on the registration city, the applicant and the stated purpose. The company may separately hold its articles, shareholder register and transaction records.

A family relationship does not by itself unlock every file. Ask who may apply and what authority must be shown before arranging death, relationship or power-of-attorney documents across borders.

3. Give every family-held clue a source and date

Put capital payments, distributions, share agreements, signatures, company emails and exchanges with other shareholders on a timeline. A title such as founder, director or general manager does not prove that the person remained a shareholder at death.

For each item, record where it came from, its date, the most it can prove and what remains unverified. If a payment, agreement and registry entry point to different percentages, preserve the conflict instead of choosing the most favourable figure.

4. Ask for the right record on the first contact

Once there is a credible ownership trail, look for the articles and shareholder register in force around the date of death. Current articles may not show an earlier capital increase, transfer or special arrangement.

On the first call, state the deceased's name, the company, the latest known year, the records already held, and whether the family is only identifying the shares or is ready to deal with the estate. Then ask which record layer is available, who should apply, and who resolves a mismatch between registration and company records.
