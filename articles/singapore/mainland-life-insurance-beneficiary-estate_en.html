Is a Mainland Life-Insurance Death Benefit Part of the Estate? Check the Beneficiary First

A death benefit under a Mainland life policy should not automatically be treated as estate property. Confirm whether a beneficiary is named, can be identified, and remains entitled when the insured dies. The Insurance Law specifies when the benefit becomes part of the estate.

Published: 2026-10-23

Find the current beneficiary record

Collect the policy, endorsements, beneficiary-change notices, and the insurer’s latest record. An old cover page may not show a later change. Check the policyholder, insured, beneficiary names, relationships, order, and shares. The Insurance Law permits a policyholder or insured to change a beneficiary by written notice, with the insurer recording the change on the policy or another document.

If the policy says “legal heirs” or gives only a relationship, do not decide entitlement from how relatives describe one another today. Give the insurer the exact wording, clarify whether the policyholder and insured were the same person, and identify when a relationship changed. Claims review depends on the contract and law, and relatives may interpret an unclear designation differently.

When the benefit is treated as estate property

Article 42 lists the relevant situations: no beneficiary was designated; the designation is unclear and the person cannot be identified; the beneficiary died before the insured and there is no replacement; or the beneficiary lost or renounced entitlement with no other beneficiary. If both die in the same event and the order cannot be established, the law presumes the beneficiary died first.

Where an identifiable beneficiary remains entitled, the death benefit is generally claimed by that person under the policy, rather than treated as the deceased person’s bank deposit. A claim still depends on coverage, proof of the insured event, any stated shares, and the insurer’s documents. This article concerns life-insurance death benefits; other policy rights may differ.

Review other policy rights separately

A policy may also involve cash value, unpaid amounts, or other contractual rights. Naming a death-benefit beneficiary answers who may claim that payment; it does not establish that every right connected with the policy falls outside the estate. Ask the insurer to explain each payment and its contractual basis separately.

A Singapore-based family dealing with a Mainland policy still needs to review the Mainland contract and insurer procedure. Prepare the policy number, proof of death, and the applicant’s identity and relationship records. Ask how overseas records must be prepared, translated, or submitted through a representative. If a named beneficiary has died, check whether the policy identifies a contingent or additional beneficiary before concluding that the benefit becomes estate property. When a name or relationship is unclear, ask the insurer for the recorded designation and change history, and review the original contract if needed. A beneficiary may also be an heir, but the two roles can differ; each has a distinct place in a claim and an estate administration.

Where the policyholder and insured are different people, verify who had authority to change the beneficiary and when the insurer received the notice.

For a Mainland policy review

  • The current policy, endorsements, and change records
  • Beneficiary identity, order, shares, and current status
  • The insurer’s explanation of payments and claimant eligibility
  • The required form for overseas death and family records
