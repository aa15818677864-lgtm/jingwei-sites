Other Mainland Estate Assets / Older or vulnerable heir

An older or cognitively impaired relative is still an heir: children cannot simply sign for them

Advanced age, a care-home address or difficulty travelling does not remove decision-making rights. The real questions are whether the person understands this decision and, only if necessary, who may act faithfully for them.

Last updated：2026-08-03

Three points to keep in view

  • Ask the person before deciding for them
  • Separate physical and communication needs from cognition
  • Check conflict when the representative also benefits

A recurring point of failure

This composite example reflects common enquiries. Mrs Lam, aged 87, lives in a Hong Kong care home. Her husband left Mainland savings and a parking space in Guangzhou. Their three children have agreed a distribution and say they only need their mother's signature. The eldest son proposes signing because she 'does not understand anything'.

Care notes show that Mrs Lam can sometimes describe her family and assets clearly, but is confused at other times. The eldest son would also receive the parking space. This is not a missing-signature problem. It requires an assessment of whether she can make this decision and who could assist without an unmanaged conflict.

The short answer: age does not prove incapacity, and kinship does not create automatic authority

If the person can understand the assets, choices and main consequences, they should be supported to speak and decide directly. Poor hearing, tremor, being bedbound or requiring interpretation is not the same as lacking understanding.

Only where the person genuinely cannot understand or communicate a stable decision should the family examine an existing guardianship or representation arrangement and whether formal confirmation is needed. A short video of a nod is rarely a substitute for a proper record of understanding and wishes.

Separate movement, communication and understanding

One person may be unable to walk but understand perfectly. Another may speak slowly yet compare options. A third may sound conversational while being unable to retain asset information or grasp a waiver. They require different responses.

Use large-print material, the person's familiar language, hearing support and shorter questions, and meet at a good time of day. Improve the conditions for communication before concluding that cognition is the problem.

When the person can decide, record more than the word 'agree'

Check that the person knows who died, the main assets, available choices and what signing will give up or secure. Legal vocabulary is unnecessary, but the answer should show a decision grounded in their own understanding.

Record the date, attendees, language, support used, the person's own explanation and any pressure. Video may support that record, but an edited clip should not hide inconsistency or replace substantive assessment.

If representation is necessary, verify authority and conflict

A child, spouse or carer does not automatically obtain power to dispose of property. Identify any existing guardianship, power or other formal arrangement and whether its scope actually covers the Mainland estate and the proposed documents.

If the representative receives the parking space, extra funds or reimbursement under the same plan, explain why the arrangement still serves the older person's interests. A different participant or a procedure with third-party scrutiny may be needed where the conflict is substantial.

Do not privately net care costs against an inheritance

A carer may have paid nursing, medical or living costs for years. Those expenses can be documented, but they do not by themselves allow the carer to waive the older person's inheritance or deduct assets without an account.

List each payment by date, purpose, payer and supporting record, then discuss reimbursement separately. Distinguishing care contribution, expense repayment and estate distribution makes the family discussion more reliable.

Five checks before any document is sent

Record the person's living and health circumstances, recent cognitive or medical information, preferred language and support, existing authority documents, and what each family member receives under the proposal.

Ask whether delay genuinely puts the asset at immediate risk. If not, convenience is not a reason to bypass the person's wishes. If rent is disappearing, accounts show unusual activity or an asset may be sold, assess preservation separately.

Five questions to ask

  • Can the person understand with suitable support?
  • Are their own choice and reasons recorded?
  • Does the proposed signer have sufficient authority?
  • Does that signer also benefit?
  • Are care costs accounted for separately from distribution?
