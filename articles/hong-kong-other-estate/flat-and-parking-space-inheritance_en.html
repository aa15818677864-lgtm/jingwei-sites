Hong Kong families / Mainland flats and parking rights

Can a Mainland flat and parking space pass together? Verify the parking right first

An access card, receipts, or a memory that the space came with the flat is not a title conclusion. First identify whether the space is separately registered, contractually tied to the flat, leased, or shared.

Last updated: 5 August 2026

Start with three points

  • Review the flat and parking records separately
  • Long use alone does not prove ownership
  • Ask whether the receiving office handles them together

The short answer: investigate both, but do not assume they are one estate asset

A numbered space may be a separately registrable unit, a facility covered by the flat purchase documents, a lease or long-term use arrangement, or a space on an area shared by all owners. That classification affects whether the parking right can be listed separately in the estate, who must participate, and what the local receiving office will ask to see.

Review the flat title record, purchase agreement and schedules, parking agreement, payment evidence, and current registration material together. An access card, management-fee receipt, or years of use may support the history of use, but none of them alone proves ownership.

A typical family problem

This is a composite example. Mr Chan's father left a flat in Shenzhen. The family found the flat's title record, a parking-use agreement, several receipts, and an access card, but no separate certificate for the parking space. One sibling remembers that the space was "bought with the flat." Another wants the family agreement to give the flat to one heir and the parking space to another.

Distribution should pause until the right is identified. If the agreement granted only a fixed-term right to use the space, the family may not be able to transfer it as a separate property. If the registry instead shows a distinct right in the father's name, dealing with the flat alone may leave an estate item unresolved.

Sort the parking space into one of four working categories

A potentially separate registered unit: look for a property-unit number, registration reference, stated area, registered holder, and any mortgage, restriction, or enforcement entry. These are stronger signs that the space needs its own estate review, although the application route still depends on the place of registration.

A space covered by the flat purchase documents: it may have been sold, included, or otherwise linked to the flat. Review the full agreement, addenda, handover list, and payment breakdown rather than relying on marketing material or family memory.

A lease, long-term use right, or other contract: check the contracting parties, duration, transfer or succession terms, unpaid charges, and termination clauses. The estate may be dealing with contractual rights and obligations rather than ownership of real property.

A space on an owner-shared area: a fixed number, an allocation by the property manager, or long use does not by itself turn a shared facility into an independently disposable asset. Management rules, owners' decisions, and allocation records matter.

Review five document groups together

Collect the current registration material for both the flat and the space: certificates, reference numbers, location, area, registered holder, and restrictions. Add the flat purchase agreement and schedules; every parking sale, inclusion, lease, or use agreement; invoices, bank transfers, management charges, rent, and access-card records; and finally the death, family relationship, will, distribution, and authority documents.

For each item, record where it came from, its date, who holds the original, and what it actually proves. Keep "the father used this space" separate from "the space was registered to the father." This prevents an assumption from becoming the basis of a family settlement.

No separate certificate does not produce one automatic answer

The absence of a separate certificate may mean that the space is recorded with the flat and is not separately certificated. It may instead mean that the space was never capable of separate registration, or that the family has only a contractual use arrangement. The label "with certificate" or "without certificate" is not enough.

Ask the local receiving office three precise questions: Does this exact space have its own registered property unit? If it is ancillary to the flat, will the inheritance application cover both together? If the available evidence is contractual only, which organisation should first confirm the contract's current status? Give the project name, space number, and document descriptions.

Prepare Hong Kong documents only after their purpose is clear

Before producing declarations or powers of attorney for every family member, identify what the receiving party is trying to verify at the current step: death, family relationship, a will, the agreed distribution, or authority to act. Hong Kong documents should then be prepared for that stated purpose.

Authority to search the flat and parking records, obtain files, submit an inheritance application, or receive correspondence should be described separately. Permission to make enquiries is not permission to sell, collect proceeds, or give up a right. Any broader authority requires its own confirmation.

If the family has not agreed, protect three things first

Preserve the current use and access record, and do not let one relative sell, sublet, or collect long-term charges privately. Record management charges and necessary preservation expenses item by item; paying a bill does not make the payer the owner. Verify the right before negotiating who receives it, even if one heir holds the access card or has used the space for years.

If a relative, tenant, or third party controls the space, keep the access-card handover history, correspondence with the property manager, payment records, and dated photographs. Whether any further step is appropriate depends on the type of right, the dispute, and the local procedure.

Pre-filing checklist

  • Does the flat's certificate or current register mention the parking space?
  • Is there a separate property-unit number, registration reference, area, or holder?
  • How do the purchase agreement, schedules, and handover list describe the space?
  • Is the agreement a sale, inclusion with the flat, lease, or use arrangement?
  • What do the payments, management charges, and access card prove?
  • Is there a mortgage, restriction, unpaid charge, tenancy, or third-party occupation?
  • Have all heirs been identified, and who may only enquire rather than decide?
  • Will the receiving office process the flat and the space together or separately?
  • Are the purpose, number, and authority scope of the Hong Kong documents confirmed?

When a focused review is useful

A focused review is sensible if the family has only an access card and receipts, the contract conflicts with the register, a third party has used the space for years, the family wants to allocate the flat and space to different people, or the receiving office says the space cannot be handled separately.

The immediate goal is not a promised result. It is to answer three practical questions: what right the father actually left, which organisation can verify it, and what step the family can safely take now.
