Other Mainland Estate Assets / Minor heir

A minor inherits a Mainland estate: a parent may act, but cannot casually give up the child's share

A child being too young to sign does not make the child's interest available for adults to park elsewhere. The first question is who may act for the child and whether the proposal genuinely protects that child.

Last updated：2026-08-03

Three points to keep in view

  • Put the child on the complete heir map
  • Test for conflict when the representative is also an heir
  • Replace family promises with an enforceable, traceable arrangement

Start with the facts

This composite example reflects recurring enquiries rather than a particular client. Mr Chan left a Shenzhen flat and bank funds. His Hong Kong wife and twelve-year-old son may inherit, and his parents are also alive. The adults propose transferring the flat to the grandfather now and paying the child after a future sale.

Everyone appears cooperative, but the wife has her own interest in the estate while proposing to sign for her son. No document fixes the child's amount, payment date or custody of the money. The danger is treating adult convenience as if it were the child's best interest.

The short answer: an appropriate adult may act, but cannot simply reduce the child's entitlement

First isolate what actually belonged to the deceased, identify every potential heir and estimate the child's interest. Only then should the family decide who is suitable to represent the child. If that person also benefits from the same arrangement, the conflict needs to be examined rather than ignored.

A proposal that makes the child waive, take less, wait for payment or rely on an asset first being transferred to another adult needs more than a family promise. Valuation, payment security, custody, registration and oversight should all be recorded. A more neutral route may be necessary where the conflict is real.

Map the child's legal position before asking anyone to sign

Draw the family tree first: spouse, children, parents, any will, and whether part of the flat already belongs to the surviving spouse. The child's possible share cannot be estimated until the deceased's own estate has been separated from other people's property.

Do not omit the child because they lack an adult identity document, cannot travel to the Mainland or do not understand the process. Birth records, identity documents, the parents' marriage records and any name changes belong on the opening document list.

A parent can assist, but cannot resolve a conflict by assertion

A suitable guardian may handle routine steps. But if the same adult is also an heir, proposed transferee or person owing compensation to the child, family status alone does not answer whether the decision is proper. The substance of the bargain must benefit the child.

If the mother receives the flat while the child gets an unsecured payment years later, test the valuation, payment protection and interim risk. An independent participant or a process that records neutral scrutiny may be needed.

Even a friendly family must define what the child will receive

The written plan should identify the asset, valuation date, child's share, proposed holder or seller, amount and payment date, and how funds will be held until adulthood. Phrases such as 'shared equally' or 'held by grandfather' are not an operational plan.

If the flat cannot be sold yet, deal with rent, repairs, mortgage payments, charges and major decisions. The child's interest includes interim income and risk, not only a final lump sum.

Four shortcuts that create lasting problems

Do not place the flat or funds in an adult's name first and promise to compensate later. Do not let a benefiting adult sign an unvalued waiver for the child. Do not mix the child's money into an ordinary household account. Do not treat a family chat message as the complete agreement.

When someone says 'sign first and sort it out later', identify what the document actually does. Confirming identity, selecting a procedure and disposing of an asset are very different acts, even if the paperwork looks routine.

Prepare a minor-heir brief before seeking advice

Collect identity, birth and relationship records; list every potential heir; locate any will; identify each asset and who holds the originals. Then write down each adult's proposed distribution and what the child receives under each version.

Add any time pressure: rent being collected, mortgage deductions, a planned sale or a bank deadline. That allows the adviser to decide whether asset preservation, representation or a reviewed distribution plan must come first.

One-minute check

  • Is the child on the complete heir list?
  • Does the proposed representative also benefit?
  • Are amount, timing and custody objectively verifiable?
  • Are interim income and expenses recorded?
  • Has any waiver or reduced share received independent scrutiny?
