Other Mainland Estate Assets / Earlier death in the family

A parent died before a grandparent: can Hong Kong grandchildren take the Mainland share?

The grandchildren are not simply added to the same row as their aunt and uncle for a new headcount. The analysis usually asks where their parent would have stood and who now represents that branch.

Last updated：2026-08-03

Three points to keep in view

  • Verify the exact order of the two deaths
  • Calculate by family branch, not a mixed headcount
  • Connect grandparent, parent and grandchildren with records

A family where the denominator is easily mistaken

This composite example reflects recurring enquiries. Mr Leung owned a Foshan flat and had two sons and a daughter. His elder son died in 2022, leaving a son and daughter in Hong Kong. Mr Leung died in 2026 and no will has been found. The younger son says only he and his sister remain, so they should divide the flat equally.

The Hong Kong grandchildren say their father would have had a share and they should take his place. The question is not whether a grandchild is ordinarily in the first class of heirs. It is whether direct descendants step into the branch of a child who died before the parent.

The short answer: the grandchildren may take their parent's branch, not new shares equal to each aunt or uncle

Identify which children were alive when the grandparent died, which child had already died and that child's direct descendants. Where the conditions are met, the grandchildren generally share the range their parent would have taken; each grandchild does not create an extra branch equal to an aunt or uncle.

A will, disputed parentage, adoption history or another relevant family member can change the analysis. Death, birth, marriage and identity-change records should be connected before anyone calculates.

Draw deaths and branches before calculating money

Place the grandparent, spouse, every child and their direct descendants on one diagram with exact death dates. A parent who died before the grandparent and one who died after the grandparent lead to different structures; approximate years are not enough.

Mark any will, waiver or disputed identity. Fixing people and time before shares prevents the family from arguing over the wrong denominator.

Think in branches rather than adding grandchildren to one row

If the grandparent had three children and the eldest died first leaving two children, the opening structure ordinarily still examines three child branches. The two grandchildren then divide the eldest child's branch rather than joining the surviving son and daughter in a four-person split.

This illustrates structure only. A spouse, will, asset ownership and special facts may alter the real result in any family.

Hong Kong grandchildren need a three-generation identity chain

The documents must connect grandparent to parent, parent to grandchildren, and establish both death dates. Birth, marriage, historic household, Hong Kong identity and name-change records may all need cross-reference.

A grandchild's Hong Kong identity card alone does not prove the three-generation chain. A parent's death certificate alone does not identify the grandchildren. Prepare a discrepancy table for names, transliterations or dates.

An uncle's family declaration cannot sign away the branch

Aunts and uncles can describe the family background, but cannot waive or collect for grandchildren merely because those grandchildren live in Hong Kong or know little about Mainland procedure. The grandchildren should know the asset, valuation, possible position and document effect.

Any power should distinguish searches, submissions, receipt of documents, negotiation, signatures, disposal and receipt of funds. Do not hide all of them in an ambiguous general authority.

Turn the branch calculation into an operational plan

After the structure is confirmed, decide who takes or sells the flat, how rent is accounted for and who advances necessary costs. Where grandchildren share one branch, specify a contact and joint-decision process.

Record acquisition method, payment and transfer sequence, custody of originals and what happens if someone cannot sign on time. A correct legal map gives the family a credible negotiation baseline.

Five checks for the earlier death

  • Exact dates of both deaths
  • Every child branch of the grandparent
  • Three-generation relationship chain
  • Any will or disputed status
  • Specific, informed authority from the grandchildren
