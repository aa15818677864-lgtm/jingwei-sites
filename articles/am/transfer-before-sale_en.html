Inherited Mainland China Property: Transfer First or Sell?

If a Macau family wants to sell a property still registered in a deceased relative's name in mainland China, do not assume the inheritance registration can be skipped. Do not assume the opposite either: local practice does not always require the family to collect a newly issued title certificate before speaking to a buyer.

Four points to remember

  • Check the registered owner, share and restrictions
  • Identify everyone whose authority must be resolved
  • Ask whether the two filings can be accepted in sequence
  • The tax review may need the original purchase records

The practical answer

The practical answer depends on the property record, the people entitled to participate, any mortgage or restriction, the local registry's intake process, and the documents available for the tax review.

If you only have 30 minutes today, do four things: write down the city and property address, identify the latest registered owner, list every family member who may be involved, and do not take a buyer's deposit yet.

Inheritance registration and a buyer transfer answer different questions

The inheritance filing establishes a registrable chain from the deceased owner to the people who will take the property. The registry may need death records, family-relationship evidence, a will or family allocation documents, and the existing title record. This article does not treat the registration date as the universal date on which every inheritance right begins.

A sale transfer is a separate transaction. It requires a person with authority to dispose of the property, a buyer, transaction documents, tax handling, and arrangements for signatures and payment.

Some local registries may accept the two filings in sequence or through a coordinated counter. That is a processing arrangement, not permission to ignore an heir, a disputed share, or a missing signature.

Start with the current property record

Before negotiating a closing date, check:

Checklist:
• the registered owner's name and identification details;
• whether the property was solely owned or co-owned;
• the deceased owner's recorded share;
• any mortgage, seizure, transfer restriction, lease, or occupier;
• the title certificate, original purchase contract, invoices, and tax records;
• any change to the address, use, floor area, or ownership history.

If the registry record and the family's understanding do not match, investigate the difference first. An old certificate is not enough reason to take a deposit or promise a transfer date.

Identify everyone whose authority must be resolved

Create one list of the possible heirs. For each person, record the relationship, location, contact status, view on the sale, and ability to attend or sign remotely.

The route may change if someone has died, is a minor, cannot be found, or disputes the proposed shares. The family member who normally handles paperwork does not automatically have authority to sell for everyone else. Do not use a generic power of attorney before the receiving registry confirms the form and signing requirements.

Ask the local registry six specific questions

Avoid the vague question, "Can we sell it directly?" Ask:

Checklist:
• Can the inheritance registration and buyer transfer be accepted as linked or consecutive applications for this property?
• Must the inheritance registration be completed and a new title document issued first?
• Which heirs, co-owners, or spouses with an actual interest in this property must appear or sign?
• What form of authorization can a person in Macau use, and who should approve the signing version?
• What must be resolved first if there is a mortgage, seizure, name mismatch, or missing original?
• Which originals, verification documents, and tax records are required for each application?

Record the date, office, property details given during the inquiry, and the names of the requested documents. One family coordinator can make the call and circulate a written note so that older relatives and family members in other cities are working from the same answer.

The tax review may depend on the deceased owner's records

Tax treatment on a later sale may require evidence of the deceased owner's original purchase date, acquisition cost, and transaction expenses. Keep the old purchase contract, invoices, tax receipts, title history, inheritance documents, and current sale-expense records.

Do not compare the two routes using a single percentage found online. Do not invent an acquisition cost when an invoice is missing. Organize the available evidence, then confirm the calculation basis with the tax office or an appropriate professional where the property is located.

When a family should avoid promising a quick sale

Pause before agreeing to a buyer's timetable if:

Checklist:
• the heirs or their shares have not been confirmed;
• the family disagrees about the sale, price, or proceeds;
• the property is mortgaged, seized, co-owned, or occupied;
• key title or tax records are missing;
• a Macau document, name link, or authorization format is still being checked;
• no one has confirmed who may sign the contract.

Completing the inheritance registration first may add a visible step, but it can reduce the much larger risk of taking a deposit and then being unable to transfer title.

Even a linked filing needs a written transaction plan

If the local registry confirms that linked or consecutive filings are available, decide before signing:

Checklist:
• who may sign for the seller and receive registry notices;
• what happens to the deposit and deadlines if the inheritance or tax review takes longer;
• which account receives the sale proceeds and how each heir approves the distribution.

There is no single sequence that fits every mainland city and every family. The defensible choice comes from this property's record, the authority of all relevant family members, the local intake route, and documents that can actually be verified.
