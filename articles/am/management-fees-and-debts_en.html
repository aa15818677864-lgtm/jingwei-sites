Mainland Property Debts: Verify the Figures Before Signing

If a Macau family receives a demand for management fees, repair charges or other debts linked to a deceased relative's Mainland property, the safer first step is usually to verify what each amount represents. Check the billing period, basis and supporting records before deciding who should sign and in what capacity.

Last updated: 2026-07-28

Four points to remember

  • A demand does not prove every figure
  • Separate ongoing charges, estate debts and advances
  • An urgent payment need not settle every dispute
  • Check capacity, scope and funding before signing

The practical answer

A demand is not proof that every figure is correct. Contacting the claimant, asking for an itemised account or making an urgent preservation payment should not be recorded loosely as an agreement to cover every liability. The wording signed, the source of payment and the family's later actions still matter.

Ask for the service agreement or other basis for the charge, a period-by-period statement, payment history, demand dates, and the calculation of any interest or late fee. If a document is presented as an acknowledgment, undertaking or repayment plan, determine whether it merely records receipt or asks the signer to admit the amount, waive objections, give security or promise personal payment.

Do not treat one total as one type of debt

A single statement may combine management fees unpaid before death, charges continuing after death, one-off repairs, late fees and amounts said to have been advanced by a relative. Those items may have different supporting records and different reasons for payment.

Start with the property address, registered owner's name, date of death and present occupation or rental status. For every amount, record:

Checklist:
• who is claiming it and whether the payee matches the agreement or statement;
• the period covered and the charging basis;
• payments already made and any receipt or bank record;
• the start date and method for interest, late fees or additions; and
• who used, rented or managed the property after the death.

If the family has only a total, request the breakdown first. Mark figures given by telephone as unverified until documents arrive. Do not place them directly into a final estate distribution calculation.

Separate estate liabilities, ongoing property costs and family advances

A personal debt incurred by the deceased should be checked against the claimant, transaction, amount, due date and evidence. Costs continuing after death may require a separate review of who was managing, occupying or preserving the property. Expenses said to have been incurred for the estate should also be tested for their purpose, supporting records and reasonableness.

A relative who advances money does not automatically acquire a larger share of the property. Keep the payment date, account, invoice, purpose and notice given to the family. Whether the amount can later be treated as an estate expense or shared by others must still be assessed.

How a beneficiary responds to a deceased person's liabilities may depend on the estate actually received and its value, as well as documents signed and steps already taken. A family member should not promise that "I will pay everything" before the estate is known. The reverse assumption is also unsafe: a debt does not disappear merely because nobody has yet signed.

An urgent payment can preserve the property without settling every dispute

Some issues cannot wait. A leak, safety concern or rapidly increasing charge may require a limited preservation step. Ask what minimum action is needed, how payment can be made properly and what receipt will be issued. Keep that decision separate from accepting all historical charges.

The family record should identify the exact item paid, the person advancing it and any periods still under review. A payment note may help explain the purpose, but no standard wording is effective in every case. Whether the payment is ultimately recoverable from the estate or shared by other family members remains a separate question.

Do not log into the deceased person's bank or payment account or use a password, one-time code or facial verification. If the previous debit route no longer works, ask the payee for a documented alternative.

Before signing, identify exactly what the document does

Ask six questions:

Checklist:
• Who requires the signature, and where will the document be used?
• Is the signer acting as a beneficiary, estate representative, authorised contact or in a personal capacity?
• Does the document acknowledge receipt only, or admit the nature and amount of the debt?
• Does it contain a personal payment promise, guarantee, waiver or other broad term?
• Is payment expected from estate assets, property income or personal funds?
• Do other beneficiaries know about the proposal, and is a joint decision or further authority required?

Macau documents concerning death, family relationship or estate authority may establish identity and capacity. They do not automatically alter a Mainland property service arrangement, the amount owed or the property registration. Ask the Mainland recipient what each document is meant to prove.

If an older relative has only the document awaiting signature, preserve every complete page and flag terms such as "amount admitted", "promise to pay", "guarantee", "waiver" and "all sums". Do not review only the signature page, and do not let one coordinator state that the whole family agrees without authority.

Use a short tracker the family can actually maintain

Begin with five columns: claimant, type of charge, billing period, amount claimed, and contractual or billing support. Once the breakdown arrives, add the verified amount, missing information or objection, person who paid, and next action.

Update only the relevant row when new material arrives, and retain dated versions. If the family disagrees, identify the disputed line. Do not combine ownership, previous payments and responsibility for family communication into one argument.

Pause before making a personal commitment when

Checklist:
• the claimant will not provide the agreement, itemised period or payment history;
• the figure mixes a mortgage, guarantee, renovation, loan or unrelated personal transaction;
• the document asks the signer to accept "all" or unspecified amounts;
• the property has several owners, tenants, an occupying relative or pending proceedings;
• a family advance is disputed or its treatment as an estate expense is unclear; or
• the estate, beneficiaries or representative's authority has not been established.

The immediate decision is not simply whether to pay. Put the basis of each charge, the condition of the property, the known estate and the proposed document side by side. That allows the family to identify what must be addressed now, what still needs evidence and who can take the next step for the estate.
