Mainland estates for Macau families

An old Mainland house has no certificate: organising historical evidence for a Macau family

A missing certificate and a property that was never registered are different problems. Before arranging inheritance documents from Macau, identify how the deceased acquired the relevant rights. Long occupation is a useful historical clue, but does not by itself establish ownership or lawful construction.

Published: 2026-10-23

Identify why there is no certificate

If a certificate was previously issued, investigate the register and historical file under the local enquiry procedure. If a purchased home was never registered, review the purchase agreement, payment, delivery and project status. The estate may involve an acquired property right or rights under a contract; these should not be described interchangeably.

For a self-built house, look for land records and construction approvals. Mainland law recognises rights arising from lawful construction in specified circumstances, but age alone does not prove lawfulness. A rented public housing unit, a rural house and an ordinary commercial apartment require different analysis.

Build three connected evidence files

The acquisition file should contain the available purchase contracts, receipts, building applications, approvals, land documents, plans or resettlement papers. Note dates and record holders. The people file should identify applicants, purchasers, registered persons, contributors and possible co-owners, with a separate explanation of changed names or identity documents.

The property file should track old and current addresses, construction and extension dates, recorded areas and present condition. Add identifiable photographs. Utility bills, management charges and neighbour statements may help explain occupation, but usually cannot establish ownership on their own. Beside each item, state the fact it supports and the question it leaves unanswered. This makes gaps clearer than a large undifferentiated bundle.

Confirm the rights before promising a registration route

Ask the competent institutions where the house is situated whether the obstacle concerns historical approvals, ownership evidence, first registration or inheritance evidence. Obtain the actual local requirements for any historical regularisation scheme. A successful example from another city is not an assurance that the same route applies.

If there is a dispute, assess its nature and the appropriate procedure. Inheritance does not automatically legalise unauthorised construction, and a court case is not a guaranteed solution to every registration obstacle. Macau family members can begin with complete copies, then arrange authority and documentary formalities once the receiving institution's requirements are clear.

Historical evidence map

  • Clues to any previous registration or certificate.
  • Purchase, construction or resettlement records.
  • People and possible co-ownership chart.
  • Address, area and building-change timeline.
  • Current condition and limits of supporting evidence.
  • Locally confirmed gaps and documentary requirements.
