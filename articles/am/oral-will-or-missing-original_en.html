No Original Will Has Been Found: What Should the Family Preserve?

Do not begin by declaring that there is, or is not, a valid will. Sort what the family actually has into four groups: a possible paper original; a complete copy or photograph; a first-hand account from someone who saw or heard what happened; and a digital trace on a phone or computer. For every item, record its source, date, custodian, and current location. Mark anything uncertain as unverified.

The practical answer

The immediate task is preservation, not a family ruling on validity. If someone is removing documents, deleting messages, withdrawing money, marketing property, or pressing others to sign, keep the notice, communication, and earliest known date. Urgent steps should then be assessed for the actual asset and place involved.

1. Describe what “missing” means before searching

Use only a description the family can support: not yet found at home; reportedly seen by a named person; possibly held by a relative, adviser, or institution; available only as a photograph or scan; known only through something the deceased said; or seen before but now of unknown whereabouts.

“Not found” is not the same as “destroyed.” Nor should a suspected custodian be described as withholding the document without evidence. Record separately who last saw it, when and where, its apparent page count, binding, signatures, and the people present. When accounts differ, preserve each account in the speaker's own words instead of merging them into one family version.

2. Build a search log and a chain of custody

List the places where the deceased kept important records: labelled folders, a safe, clues to a safe-deposit box, email attachments, cloud storage, old phones, computers, and messaging accounts. Identify anyone who may have helped draft, print, witness, translate, send, or store the document. Ask whether any version was lodged with a court, notarial office, bank, property office, company, or adviser.

For each search, record the date, participants, scope, and result. If paper is found, do not remove staples, rebind pages, annotate it, or take only the pages concerning one Mainland asset. Photograph its condition and location before moving it, then record discovery and handover. Do not access another person's account, unlock a device without authority, or take material held by someone else.

3. A photograph is a lead; preserve its provenance

A scan or photograph may help locate the paper, but it should not be renamed “the original.” Keep the entire file, original filename, received date, sender, surrounding conversation, and every available page. Do not crop out only a signature or the clause that appears favourable. For a group chat, retain the group identity, sending account, and full context.

Create a copy-source sheet: who made the image, which device was used, whether that person saw the paper, who held the paper at the time, and whether the file was forwarded, compressed, or exported again. Leave an answer blank when it is unknown. The sheet does not turn a copy into an original; it makes the route from paper to copy traceable.

4. For an oral account, record the event rather than a conclusion

Do not ask a witness to decide whether the words “count as a will.” Record when and where the deceased spoke, the circumstances, everyone present, who heard the words directly, who learned of them later, the substance of the words, any asset identified, and any related recording, message, medical record, or itinerary. Also record whether the deceased later wrote, printed, recorded, confirmed, or changed the arrangement.

Oral arrangements are subject to strict, fact-sensitive conditions. The urgency of the situation, the number and identity of people present, their interest in the estate, and later events may all matter. Hearing a wish about distribution does not establish that every condition was met. Each direct listener should check a separate account. The family should not rehearse a common statement or make a new “witness video” after the event.

5. Keep the original device and the whole digital context

If a phone contains a voice note, chat, email, photograph, or document, pause unnecessary clean-up and device migration. Record the device model, custodian, account name, location of the material, and discovery date. Preserve enough of the account and conversation to identify the participants, timestamps, surrounding messages, and the opened attachment.

Screenshots may be useful working copies, but do not delete the live conversation after taking them or select only favourable messages. Keep the audio after making a transcript. When creating a handover copy, retain an unedited version and note who made it, when, and how. Whether formal preservation is needed depends on the dispute risk and the procedure that may follow.

6. Separate existence, authenticity, and legal effect

The first question is whether a particular document existed, where it may be, and whether its source can be traced. Separate questions concern how it was made, whether it is complete, whether a later version exists, and how it may be treated in the relevant process. A family can organise the first set of facts; handwriting, image quality, or one relative's confidence cannot settle the rest.

Keep handwritten, printed, audio, and differently dated versions in their original condition and place each on the timeline. Do not discard an older or unfavourable version, or one that does not mention the Mainland asset. Never assemble clauses from several files into a supposedly complete will.

7. List the Mainland assets while pausing rights-changing acts

Give each property, account, company interest, or receivable its own row. Record the city, registered name, known reference, present condition, document custodian, and immediate risk. While the will search remains open, preserving mortgage, management-fee, rent, company, and bank records is usually more useful than rushing into distribution.

Label each proposed act as preservation, information gathering, or a change of rights. Photographs, inventories, and retained bills fall into the first two groups. A sale, lease, withdrawal, transfer, waiver, or distribution agreement may change rights. Before taking such an act, identify who can decide, the current succession position, and what the actual receiving institution requires.

8. Prepare one handover sheet

Checklist:
• the deceased's names, date of death, and recent places of life;
• the reported time, place, method, and circumstances of the will;
• the source of every paper, image, scan, recording, or message;
• when and where the original was last seen, and by whom;
• each direct listener's identity, relationship, contact details, and account;
• the custodian, account, and data location for every relevant device;
• the date, pages, signatures, witness details, and differences for every version;
• each place or institution searched, with date, reply, and next step;
• the city, registered name, condition, and urgent risk for each Mainland asset; and
• questions that remain unknown and require institutional or professional checking.

Limit the sheet to necessary information. Identity numbers, complete account numbers, private conversations, and medical material should go only to people who need them, not a broad family chat.

9. A practical first step for five kinds of reader

A first-time organiser should create the four evidence groups and one timeline. Someone holding only a photograph should preserve the complete file and transmission context, then identify who saw the paper. An older relative only needs to answer four questions: who spoke, when and where, who was present, and where the material might be.

In a divided family, take separate accounts and do not let one contact rewrite everyone else's memory. Preserve written requests and replies without taking jointly held material. A person preparing for advice should bring every version, the copy-source sheet, original devices, witness list, search log, timeline, and asset-risk table.

10. Use this order

First classify the situation: a paper original not yet found, only a copy, only an oral account, or a digital trace. Next identify the last custodian, search scope, direct listeners, and full timeline. Preserve original devices and all versions while listing immediate risks to each Mainland asset. Once provenance and facts are clearer, ask the actual recipient or an adviser which record, version, and procedural step is required.

This article does not decide whether a will is valid. It addresses the earlier and more practical problem: preserving the people, papers, devices, and timeline that may otherwise disappear before the family can obtain a proper assessment.
